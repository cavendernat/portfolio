Project views

Investigating how a research repository was actually being used

During my NCDS internship with MIT Libraries, I conducted an end-to-end analysis of web traffic for a research data repository. I worked with approximately 11,000 recorded sessions and investigated how researchers and other visitors interacted with the site, which resources attracted attention, how users navigated the repository, and how much of the apparent activity represented genuine human use rather than automated traffic.

Data cleaning & behavioral analysis

I transformed raw web analytics logs into data that could be used to study user behavior. I analyzed metrics including session duration, pages per session, time spent on individual pages, new versus returning visitors, entry and exit behavior, search activity, and traffic patterns across different days and times. This required cleaning and restructuring the underlying data before meaningful behavioral patterns could be identified.

Identifying human and automated traffic

One of the central challenges was determining whether repository traffic came from actual users or automated systems. Simple traffic counts could substantially overstate engagement if bots, crawlers, and other automated requests were treated as human visitors. I developed behavioral rules for distinguishing likely human sessions from automated activity using combinations of session duration, navigation behavior, page counts, and other available signals.

Exploring machine learning approaches

I also investigated whether unsupervised machine learning could reveal behavioral groups that were difficult to identify with fixed rules alone. I experimented with clustering techniques such as K-means and dimensional-reduction methods including UMAP and t-SNE to explore patterns across session behavior. I compared these approaches with rule-based classification to understand both their potential and their limitations for identifying unusual or automated traffic.

Data visualization

I created visualizations to make repository behavior easier to interpret, including traffic distributions, engagement patterns, and heatmaps showing activity by hour and day of the week. I also explored interactive visualizations that allowed different behavioral thresholds to be adjusted dynamically, making it possible to see how changing the definition of likely human activity affected the resulting analysis.

From analysis to recommendations

I connected the findings to practical recommendations for improving repository analytics and reducing the influence of automated traffic. I evaluated approaches including robots.txt policies, COUNTER-aligned reporting, firewall controls, Cloudflare and CAPTCHA-based protections, and alternative methods for measuring meaningful repository engagement. The goal was not simply to remove bots, but to help the organization understand what its analytics could reliably say about real users.

Communicating the research

I was responsible for carrying the project from exploratory analysis through final communication. I synthesized the methodology, findings, limitations, and recommendations into a detailed written report and a 30-minute presentation. This required translating a technically complex analysis into findings that could support decisions about repository analytics, infrastructure, and future measurement practices.
